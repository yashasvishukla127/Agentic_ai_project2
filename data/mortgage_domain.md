## Part 1 — Loan Types

### Conventional Loans

A conventional loan is any mortgage not insured or guaranteed by a federal government agency. They are the most common loan type in the US market, originated and backed by private lenders such as banks, credit unions, and mortgage companies.

Conventional loans are divided into conforming and non-conforming. Conforming loans meet the purchase guidelines set by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac — the two government-sponsored enterprises that buy mortgages from lenders in the secondary market. The key conforming criteria are the loan limit (set annually by the Federal Housing Finance Agency — $766,550 for single-unit properties in 2024 in most areas, with higher limits in high-cost counties), the maximum debt-to-income ratio (typically 45% back-end DTI), and minimum credit score (typically 620, though 740+ receives the best pricing).

Non-conforming loans — commonly called jumbo loans when the issue is loan size — exceed the conforming limit and must be held on the lender's balance sheet or sold to private investors. They typically require stronger credit profiles (720+ is common), higher reserves, and carry slightly higher rates because they cannot be sold to Fannie or Freddie.

**Down payment requirements for conventional loans.** The minimum is 3% for first-time homebuyers through specific programs (Fannie Mae HomeReady, Freddie Mac Home Possible). Standard conventional loans require 5% minimum. Down payments below 20% trigger private mortgage insurance (PMI), which protects the lender if the borrower defaults. PMI is cancelled automatically once the loan balance reaches 80% of the original appraised value (per the Homeowners Protection Act) — the borrower can also request cancellation at 80% LTV based on current value through a new appraisal.

### FHA Loans

FHA loans are insured by the Federal Housing Administration, a division of the Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD). Because the federal government insures the lender against default loss, lenders are willing to accept lower credit scores and down payments than conventional loans allow.

**Minimum requirements.** Credit score of 580 or higher: minimum 3.5% down payment. Credit score between 500 and 579: minimum 10% down payment. Credit scores below 500 are not eligible for FHA financing.

**Mortgage insurance on FHA loans.** All FHA loans carry two forms of mortgage insurance. The upfront mortgage insurance premium (UFMIP) is 1.75% of the base loan amount, typically financed into the loan. The annual mortgage insurance premium (MIP) ranges from 0.15% to 0.75% of the loan balance depending on loan term, LTV, and loan amount, paid monthly. Unlike conventional PMI, FHA MIP on loans with less than 10% down payment is permanent for the life of the loan — it does not cancel at 80% LTV. Borrowers who put down 10% or more can have MIP removed after 11 years. This makes the long-term cost of FHA financing higher than conventional once sufficient equity is built.

**FHA loan limits** are set by county and cannot exceed the conforming loan limit. For 2024, the national floor is $498,257 for a single-unit property and the ceiling is $1,149,825 in the highest-cost areas.

**Eligible property types.** FHA will insure financing on one-to-four unit primary residences only. The property must meet FHA minimum property standards — a separate appraisal requirement from conventional that evaluates health and safety conditions, not just market value.

### VA Loans

VA loans are guaranteed by the US Department of Veterans Affairs and are available to eligible veterans, active-duty service members, and surviving spouses. The VA guarantee allows lenders to offer highly favourable terms.

**Key features.** No down payment required (100% financing). No private mortgage insurance. No minimum credit score set by the VA, though lenders typically apply a 620 minimum. The funding fee ranges from 1.25% to 3.3% of the loan amount depending on down payment and whether it is the borrower's first use of the benefit — this fee may be waived for veterans with a service-connected disability. Loan limits: veterans with full entitlement have no loan limit. Eligible only for primary residences.

### USDA Loans

USDA loans are guaranteed by the United States Department of Agriculture under the Rural Development program. They are designed to promote homeownership in eligible rural and suburban areas. Like VA loans, they offer 100% financing.

**Eligibility requirements.** The property must be in a USDA-eligible area (the USDA maintains an online eligibility map). Household income must not exceed 115% of the area median income. Credit score: USDA does not set a minimum, but lenders typically require 640 for automated underwriting. An annual guarantee fee of 0.35% of the outstanding loan balance is charged, plus an upfront guarantee fee of 1% of the loan amount.

## Part 2 — Loan-to-Value Ratio (LTV)

### Definition and Calculation

Loan-to-value ratio is the relationship between the loan amount and the appraised value of the property. It is expressed as a percentage.

LTV = (Loan Amount / Appraised Value) × 100

Example: A borrower purchases a home appraised at $400,000 with a $320,000 loan. LTV = 320,000 / 400,000 = 80%.

LTV is one of the most consequential variables in mortgage pricing and eligibility. It measures the lender's exposure — if the borrower defaults and the lender must foreclose, a higher LTV means less equity cushion between the loan balance and what the property sells for at auction.

### LTV Thresholds and Their Effects

**80% LTV** is the standard threshold for conventional loans. At or below 80% LTV, no private mortgage insurance is required. Rates are also more favourable at lower LTVs. Borrowers often target exactly 80% LTV to eliminate PMI cost.

**90% LTV** (10% down) on a conventional loan triggers PMI and a slightly higher rate due to increased risk.

**95% LTV** (5% down) is the standard conventional minimum for non-first-time buyers. PMI is required. Rate adjustments (loan-level price adjustments, or LLPAs, from Fannie/Freddie) increase cost.

**96.5% LTV** is the FHA standard minimum (3.5% down). The combination of UFMIP and annual MIP makes the effective cost higher than the headline rate suggests.

**100% LTV** is available only through VA and USDA programs.

### Combined Loan-to-Value (CLTV)

When a borrower has both a first mortgage and a second mortgage (home equity loan or HELOC), the combined loan-to-value ratio measures total encumbrance against the property. CLTV = (First Mortgage Balance + Second Mortgage Balance) / Appraised Value. Lenders use CLTV to evaluate risk when a second lien exists or is being added.

## Part 3 — Credit and Debt-to-Income Requirements

### Credit Score Tiers and Pricing Impact

Mortgage pricing uses tiered credit score buckets. The most commonly used scores are FICO Score 2 (Experian), FICO Score 5 (Equifax), and FICO Score 4 (TransUnion). On a joint application, lenders use the lower of the two middle scores.

Standard pricing tiers:
- 760 and above: best available pricing, minimal loan-level price adjustments
- 740–759: minimal adjustments
- 720–739: modest adjustments
- 700–719: moderate adjustments begin
- 680–699: meaningful adjustments, particularly at higher LTVs
- 660–679: significant adjustments
- 640–659: eligible for conventional but with substantial cost impact
- 620–639: at the minimum threshold for most conventional lenders; significant pricing penalty
- Below 620: ineligible for conventional; FHA accessible above 500; VA and USDA lender overlays often require 620

A credit score difference of 40 points at a given LTV can change the rate by 0.25% to 0.75% depending on market conditions and program.

### Debt-to-Income Ratio (DTI)

DTI measures a borrower's total monthly debt obligations relative to gross monthly income. There are two components.

**Front-end DTI (housing ratio)** includes only the proposed housing payment: principal, interest, property taxes, homeowner's insurance, and HOA dues if applicable. Divided by gross monthly income. Conventional guidelines: typically 28% maximum, though automated underwriting systems (AUS) may approve higher.

**Back-end DTI (total DTI)** includes the housing payment plus all recurring monthly debt obligations: auto loans, student loans (the minimum required payment or 1% of the outstanding balance if in deferment, depending on loan type), credit card minimum payments, and any other installment debt. Divided by gross monthly income.

Maximum DTI by loan type:
- Conventional (automated underwriting): up to 45–50% with compensating factors
- FHA: up to 43% manually underwritten; up to 50–57% with automated approval and compensating factors
- VA: 41% is the guideline, though lenders commonly approve higher with residual income analysis
- USDA: 29% front-end / 41% back-end standard; automated underwriting may allow higher

**Compensating factors** that allow higher DTI approval: large cash reserves (12+ months PITI), significant residual income (VA), substantial down payment, or excellent credit score.

## Part 4 — Interest Rates and Rate Locks

### What Determines a Mortgage Rate

A borrower's mortgage rate is not a single market rate — it is a composite of the baseline market rate adjusted for risk factors specific to that borrower and transaction.

**Market baseline** is driven primarily by the 10-year US Treasury yield. Mortgage rates typically trade at a spread above the 10-year Treasury — historically 1.5% to 2%, though the spread widens during periods of economic uncertainty.

**Loan-level price adjustments (LLPAs)** are add-on costs applied by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac (and priced into rates by lenders) based on credit score, LTV, loan purpose (purchase vs. refinance), occupancy type (primary residence vs. investment), and property type. These adjustments are published in the LLPA matrix and can total 0.5% to 3%+ of the loan amount in higher-risk scenarios, which translates directly into a higher rate or higher upfront cost.

**Lender margin** is the spread the individual lender adds for their own cost of origination and profit.

### Fixed vs. Adjustable Rate Mortgages

**Fixed-rate mortgages (FRM)** carry the same interest rate for the life of the loan. The most common terms are 30-year and 15-year. The 30-year fixed offers lower monthly payments but higher total interest paid over the life of the loan. The 15-year fixed has a higher monthly payment but typically a lower rate (0.5%–0.75% lower than 30-year) and significantly less total interest. Fixed rates protect the borrower from rate increases but do not benefit from decreases without refinancing.

**Adjustable-rate mortgages (ARM)** carry a fixed rate for an initial period, then adjust periodically based on an index (commonly SOFR — Secured Overnight Financing Rate, which replaced LIBOR). Common structures are 5/1, 7/1, and 10/1 — the first number is the fixed period in years, the second is the adjustment frequency. After the fixed period, the rate adjusts by the index movement subject to caps: periodic cap (maximum change per adjustment, commonly 2%), lifetime cap (maximum total change from initial rate, commonly 5%). ARMs are appropriate when the borrower plans to sell or refinance before the adjustment period begins, when they believe rates will fall, or when the initial rate savings are material enough to justify the risk.

### Rate Locks

A rate lock is a lender commitment to hold a specific rate for a defined period, protecting the borrower from rate increases between application and closing. Standard lock periods are 30, 45, and 60 days. Longer lock periods cost more — either through a higher rate or an upfront lock fee.

**Float down options** allow the borrower to reduce their locked rate if market rates fall during the lock period, subject to eligibility rules (typically rates must fall by at least 0.25% and the float down must be requested at least 10 days before closing).

**Lock expiration** is a material risk in purchase transactions. If the transaction does not close before the lock expires, the lender either extends the lock (for a fee) or the rate resets to current market. Borrowers should align the lock period with realistic closing timelines.

## Part 5 — The Underwriting Process

### Automated Underwriting Systems (AUS)

The vast majority of mortgage applications are run through one of two automated underwriting systems before human review: Fannie Mae's Desktop Underwriter (DU) or Freddie Mac's Loan Product Advisor (LPA). The AUS evaluates the file against guidelines and returns one of three findings:

**Approve/Eligible (DU) or Accept (LPA):** The loan meets guidelines and is eligible for purchase. The file goes to a human underwriter for documentation verification, not for credit analysis.

**Refer with Caution:** The file has risk factors that require manual underwriting. A human underwriter evaluates against manual guidelines, which are generally more restrictive (lower DTI limits, more compensating factors required).

**Ineligible:** The loan does not meet the program's basic requirements.

### The Three C's of Underwriting

Human underwriters evaluate files against three dimensions.

**Capacity** — can the borrower afford the payment? Assessed through income documentation (W-2s, tax returns, pay stubs, bank statements for self-employed borrowers), employment history (two-year employment history required; gaps must be explained), and DTI calculation.

**Capital** — does the borrower have sufficient assets? This includes the down payment, closing costs, and reserves. Reserves are expressed in months of PITI (principal, interest, taxes, insurance). Conventional loans generally require two months reserves; jumbo and higher-risk loans may require 12 months or more. All funds must be sourced and seasoned — gift funds require a gift letter; large deposits require written explanation and documentation.

**Credit** — what is the borrower's history of repaying debt? This goes beyond the credit score: the underwriter examines payment history, collections, judgments, bankruptcies, and derogatory marks. A bankruptcy requires a mandatory waiting period before eligibility: Chapter 7 BK requires four years from discharge for conventional (two years for FHA and VA); Chapter 13 BK requires two years from discharge for conventional (one year for FHA with court permission). Foreclosures require seven years from completion for conventional (three years for FHA).

### Self-Employed Borrower Income Documentation

Self-employed borrowers are defined as those who own 25% or more of a business or whose primary income is from self-employment. Qualifying income for self-employed borrowers uses a two-year average of net income from Schedule C (sole proprietor), Schedule K-1 (partnership or S-Corp), or W-2 wages from their own corporation — depending on the business structure. Non-recurring income (one-time items, capital gains) is typically excluded. Declining income between year one and year two creates scrutiny — underwriters may use the lower year or average depending on the magnitude of the decline.

## Part 6 — Mortgage Compliance and Disclosure Requirements

### TRID — The TILA-RESPA Integrated Disclosure Rule

TRID (effective October 2015) consolidated the disclosures previously required under the Truth in Lending Act (TILA) and the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act (RESPA) into two standardised forms.

**The Loan Estimate (LE)** must be delivered to the borrower within three business days of receiving a complete application (defined as receipt of six pieces of information: borrower name, income, social security number, property address, estimated property value, and loan amount). The LE provides a standardised summary of loan terms, projected payments, and estimated closing costs.

**The Closing Disclosure (CD)** must be provided at least three business days before consummation (closing). The three-business-day waiting period is mandatory and cannot be waived except in a bona fide personal financial emergency.

**Tolerance categories for closing costs.** TRID created three categories of closing cost tolerance:
- Zero tolerance: costs that cannot increase from LE to CD. Includes lender fees (origination, processing, underwriting), transfer taxes, and services the borrower was not allowed to shop for.
- 10% tolerance: aggregate increase across this category cannot exceed 10% from LE to CD. Includes recording fees and third-party services where the borrower selects from the lender's approved list.
- No tolerance: costs that can increase without limit. Includes homeowner's insurance, prepaid interest, and services the borrower chose outside the lender's list.

### Annual Percentage Rate (APR)

APR is a standardised cost measure required under TILA that includes not just the interest rate but most fees associated with the loan (origination fees, points, mortgage insurance). APR allows consumers to compare loan offers on a consistent basis. Because APR spreads upfront costs over the life of the loan, it always equals or exceeds the stated interest rate and can be significantly higher on loans with substantial upfront fees. For short-term holders (those who will sell or refinance before maturity), the true cost comparison is better made using annual percentage yield or a break-even analysis.

### Qualified Mortgage (QM) Rule

The Qualified Mortgage rule, established under the Dodd-Frank Act, defines a category of loans that receive a legal safe harbour for lenders — protection against claims that the lender failed to verify the borrower's ability to repay. Key QM requirements: the back-end DTI cannot exceed 43% (with exceptions for loans eligible for purchase by Fannie/Freddie under a temporary provision), no negative amortisation, no interest-only periods, no loan terms exceeding 30 years, and points and fees cannot exceed 3% of the loan amount.

Loans that do not meet QM standards are non-QM loans. They are legal and originated commonly for borrowers who do not fit agency guidelines (recently self-employed, investor borrowers, borrowers with prior credit events outside the waiting periods). Non-QM loans carry higher rates and are held on portfolio or sold to private investors.
